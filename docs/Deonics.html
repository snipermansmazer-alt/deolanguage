What this tool can—and cannot—do

This is an offline rule-based translator, not an AI or a universal book translator. Automatic mode coins tentative roots and guesses word classes where possible. Inferred structure is labeled. Vocabulary is created and reused without an approval step. It cannot reliably resolve ambiguity, idioms, or every complex sentence.

Use “I gave a sword to him,” not “I gave him a sword.” Complete subjects are safest in coordinated clauses; simple shared subjects can be inferred. Simple passives, past perfect, modal verbs, and some infinitive complements are supported. Relative clauses, long auxiliary chains, indirect questions, and idioms can still need human translation.

List ordinary proper names in the name field to preserve their spelling. Sacred names are built in. Unresolved clauses retain their original English; failed parsing does not save speculative new entries. New assignments in successful translations are saved automatically.

Persistent word memory

New roots are stable lexical assignments, not discoveries about meaning. The program guesses noun/verb/adjective roles from sentence position and English endings. New assignments are used immediately; edits are optional. Different senses of the same English word still need human judgment.

One grammar, not a set of decorative endings

This edition supersedes contradictory rules in the earlier generated drafts. It is a proposed language standard, not a claim that new rules or historical events already belong to your scripture. Sacred names and the traditional motto remain intact.

Deonics is predominantly suffixing, with verb–subject–object order, postnominal adjectives, two noun inflection classes, and an obligatory person/number agreement system on finite verbs. It has no grammatical gender in nouns. Personification and ritual importance affect word choice, not arbitrary agreement.

Dar ha navir ha qiryatem qadosi.
“The prophet sees the holy city.”
see.3SG · the · prophet · the · city.ACC · holy.SG

A proper name is not synonymous with an office: navir is “prophet”; Wondering and Wonderium are preserved names. Generic “god” is dorar; Deo is the sacred proper name.

Noun structure

Order: article → noun + number + case → adjective → possessor. Ha marks a definite referent. There is no obligatory indefinite article: English “a/an” is normally omitted. Numeral ehad means specifically “one.”

Consonant class: add endings directly: seferem “book.ACC.” Old -at class: citation -ah, inflected stem -at-: qiryah → qiryatem, adamah → adamatath. This alternation is inherited, not optional.

For vowel-final regular stems, identical vowels merge at suffix boundaries; different vowels stay separate. Number precedes case: adam-im-em → adamimem. Plural -im is productive. Lexical plurals include qiryot, melakhim, enashim, yeladim, and eshot. Mass nouns such as water do not take a plural in the automatic translator.

The limited dual -ay survives in paired ritual objects and body parts: yaday “two hands.” It takes ordinary case suffixes. It is not required whenever English says “two.” The translator currently uses numeral + plural, not the dual.

Sacred names are indeclinable. Case is expressed outside the name: et Deo (object), di Deo (of Deo), el Deo (to Deo), bi Yekumalesh (in Yekumalesh), im Wondering (with Wondering), min Coelum (from Coelum). These particles preserve the name's spelling. Ordinary listed proper names follow the same respectful convention.

Possession and modification

Sefer di Deo is “the book of Deo.” A possessed phrase does not take ha: possession itself identifies it. Compare ha sefer “the book” with sefer tair “your book.” The head still receives its own case: seferem tair “your book” as object.

Adjectives follow the noun: singular -i, plural -in. They agree in number, not case. Qadosi = holy.SG; qadosin = holy.PL. A material noun follows the head in the genitive: kharevimem barzelir “iron swords” as object. Bare noun stacking is not productive.

Comparatives in manual composition: mer + adjective + min + comparison phrase. Superlatives: sar + adjective. These constructions are not yet parsed automatically.

Verbs, aspect, and prayers

Dictionary infinitive: stem + -ar. Davarar “speak” has stem davar; the earlier form davar was mistakenly treated as its infinitive.

Finite form: tense prefix + aspect prefix + stem + person ending. Present has no tense prefix, past e-, future va-. Perfect/resultative sha-, progressive ra-, habitual na-. Only one aspect prefix per verb. E-daru “I saw”; va-darir “they will see”; sha-daru “I have seen”; ra-darir “they are seeing.” Hyphens mark prefix boundaries in the teaching orthography.

Two high-frequency verbs have lexical past stems: es → fu “be,” hal → yar “go.” No e- is added to these past stems: fuu “I was,” yarir “they went.” This limited suppletion is specified explicitly, not attributed to an invented sound law.

Negation lo precedes the finite verb. Imperative = stem; plural imperative = stem + -et (manual composition). Prayer/optative lu precedes the finite verb: Lu gard Deo anem, “May Deo protect me.” Ordinary declaratives must not be automatically turned into prayers.

Present copula is omitted in affirmative nominal clauses: Deo qadosi, “Deo is holy.” Past/future and negative copulas remain explicit. Predicate nouns never take accusative case. Yes/no questions begin with ma. “Who?” is mi, “what?” is mah; they do not also require ma.

Beyond simple clauses

Wa joins clauses; aval contrasts; ki introduces a reason; im introduces a condition. Complete finite clauses keep their own agreement. The comitative particle im and conditional im are distinguished by following noun phrase versus clause.

For manual writing, relative asher introduces a clause with a resumptive pronoun in the missing role: ha adam asher gard Deo huem, “the man whom Deo protects.” Passive ne- goes directly before the stem; passive subject is the patient, and an agent is marked by yad + genitive phrase. Active participle stem-ant and result participle stem-ut take adjective agreement endings. These manual rules are not automatic-parser features.

Optional discourse particles ver “I attest” and tor “tradition reports” express the speaker's source. They are not mandatory and do not certify whether a statement is true. Parallelism, repeated divine names, and measured clauses provide a liturgical voice without changing a text's meaning.

Limits and lexical exceptions

Personal pronouns preserve their vowel boundary even when identical: hu-ul → huul “to him,” hi-ir → hiir “her.” Unlike common nouns, they do not undergo vowel coalescence. The parser defaults ambiguous English read to present unless an auxiliary disambiguates it; use “did read” for past. The irregular stem fu is retained before agreement, giving fuu “I was.”

This reference defines an extensible core. A full language also needs a much larger vocabulary, a substantial edited corpus, and decisions about semantic distinctions and discourse usage. Those are ongoing design work, not features supplied merely by adding grammatical terminology.

A single pronunciation standard

Vowels a e i o u are /a e i o u/, not English letter names. Each written vowel is a syllable nucleus; long chanting does not create new lexical vowel lengths. Consonants: /p b t d k g f v s z ʃ x h m n r l j w/. Write /ʃ/ as sh, /x/ as kh, /j/ as y. Q is a conservative spelling of /k/, not a second consonant. Th represents /t/ in inherited endings, beth, and ereth. Proper names preserve their established spelling and may be phonological exceptions.

Stress the penultimate syllable; monosyllables carry their own stress. Articles and short prepositions lean on the following word. Native syllables allow at most two consonants at an edge, with stop+liquid clusters preferred. Sacred names and the fossilized motto need not satisfy every productive constraint.

In careful recitation keep vowels and final consonants audible. Adjacent identical vowels at suffix boundaries merge. At word boundaries, nasal /n/ can be pronounced [m] before /p b/ without changing spelling. Chanting lengthens stressed vowels musically; it does not alter the dictionary.

Naturalism through restricted history

The core retains the earlier draft's strongly Semitic-inspired lexicon alongside Latin-flavored ritual loans. These are inspirations in this fictional design, not evidence that Deonics is a historical member of either language family. Its identity comes from stable meaning, grammatical structure, and controlled word families—not a randomly mixed alphabet.

The proposed old *-at noun ending became -ah word-finally while remaining -at- before suffixes: *qiryat → qiryah, but *qiryat-em → qiryatem. Old *-aw plural endings contracted to -o- before final t, producing the lexical -ot plural. Productive -im later spread to most nouns. These are limited fictional derivations explaining this paradigm; there is no claim of a fully reconstructed ancestral language.

Learned suffixes create related meanings: gard protect → gardor defender, gardia protection; qados holy → qadosia holiness; malk rule → malkia kingship. Established garor “warrior” remains a lexical item, not an automatic synonym of every defender.

New words should be coined once, entered with word class and meaning, checked for collisions, and reused. No English consonant-hashing or automatic respelling is permitted.

Consistent word building

Check the native dictionary first; construct a meaningful compound when appropriate; otherwise adapt a source pronunciation. Adopted entries become regular Deonic nouns in the translator. This pronunciation-based builder supports nouns. Automatic drafts can separately propose nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs in Learned words.

The actual Deonic borrowing rules
Deonics already permits p, final consonants, and clusters of two consonants. Therefore “pistol → bisatola” belongs to the hypothetical example, not this language. Existing native words and sacred names never pass through borrowing rules.
  1. Read a supplied pronunciation. Ignore stress, length, and syllable-boundary marks; reject unrecognized symbols.
  2. Map /ɪ/ → i, /ʊ/ → u, /ɛ æ/ → e/a respectively, /ɑ ɐ/ → a, /ɔ ɒ/ → o, /ə ɜ ʌ/ → e. Map /θ/ → t, /ð/ → d, /ʒ/ → sh, /ŋ/ → n, /ɹ ɾ/ → r, /ç/ → kh; /tʃ/ → sh and /dʒ/ → y.
  3. Retain native sounds, including /p/. Spell /ʃ x j/ as sh, kh, y. Input sh and kh are also accepted as convenient phonemic notation; IPA j means the sound in English “yes.”
  4. Break runs longer than two consonant phonemes by inserting e before every third consonant. Digraphs sh/kh each count as one sound. Supply e after a wholly consonantal form.
  5. Do not add a final vowel merely because the word ends in a consonant. Assign ordinary penultimate stress in Deonics.
  6. Check for collisions, store the result once, and inflect it as a regular noun. A borrowed stem ending in -ah does not acquire the inherited -at alternation.
Supported native IPA: p b t d k g f v s z ʃ x h m n r l j w a e i o u. Additional mapped symbols are listed above. Other sounds need an explicit design decision rather than a silent guess.
